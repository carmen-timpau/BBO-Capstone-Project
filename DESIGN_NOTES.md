_Choice of Main Machine Learning Library_

The choice to focus preponderantly on leveraging scikit-learn’s ML tools instead of leveraging deep learning frameworks like PyTorch and TensorFlow for neural networks as part of this Black-Box Optimisation (BBO) Capstone Project was made based on the small sizes (10-40 initial datapoints) of the functions’ datasets. This aspect makes the employment of neural networks particularly unsuitable for modelling the unknown functions, as well as for effective classification purposes. This is because neural networks require massive datasets for effective training and to avoid overfitting and generalise well, while scikit-learn is best suited for small-sized datasets, like the ones available for this project.

Scikit-learn provides machine learning tools for predictive data analysis that are ideal for small-sized and low-to-medium dimensionality (2D-8D) functions. The library enables direct access to the incredibly useful GaussianProcessRegressor model, which was used heavily over the course of the whole capstone project. I chose to base my surrogate modelling on Gaussian Processes methodology for my developing Bayesian Optimisation framework, as I consider GPs to be the best surrogate models for the challenge at hand. This is because GPs show excellent performance on small datasets, offer a kernel-based structure and allow ARD feature relevance optimisation (length scales computed per dimension) and, lastly, it allows for posterior mean and variance computation, the latter being completely mandatory to allow the use of acquisition functions, which represent an essential part of BO. If my datasets would have been much larger, I would have considered the use of PyTorch or TensorFlow, but in this specific case, I did not think it would be the best option.

_Main Trade-off_: As no dedicated Bayesian Optimisation (BO) libraries like BoTorch or GPyTorch were used and instead all components of a BO pipeline were built from scratch, full visibility was achieved, as well as complete architectural and tuning control over every step within the developed ML pipelines, and no dependency on a framework’s internal assumptions, but at the expense of a larger codebase to build, develop and maintain.

_Software Architecture Decisions_

My approach for the BBO capstone project was developing a BO pipeline from scratch, rather than relying on already-built and optimised BO frameworks like BOTorch, to challenge myself and try to bring additional valuable insights to the field of Bayesian Optimisation research, while focusing on aiming to achieve the overall goal of global function maximisation and making the most out of the limited budget available within the Black-Box Optimisation capstone project challenge. 

While Weeks 1-5 represented an exploratory initial phase, where the behaviour of the unknown functions was investigated through manual model hyperparameter tuning, the approach I employed and tested in Week 6 of my capstone project can be described component-wise: an initial Gaussian Process (GP, surrogate model) kernel ablation study per function, a following acqusition function ablation study per function, which utilises the best-performing ‘winning’ GP kernel for each function, next-query prediction using the ‘winning’ GP kernel and the ‘winning’ acqusition function per function, and an additional diagnostic home/heteroscedasticity Breusch-Pagan test per function with plotting. The latter function variance study was performed to observe and attempt to characterise the intrinsic behaviour of each black-box function, so that any identified heteroscedasticity can be properly addressed in future pipelines and ensure it does not interfere with the performance of the GP surrogate model, which always assumes homoscedastic function behaviour. 

My choices for the BBO Week 6 Bayesian Optimisation pipeline architecture development and diagnostic homo/heteroscedasticity testing are both supported and have been inspired by recent relevant literature which provided important insights into what truly matters and makes a difference for a successful BO approach, as well as the factors that can interfere with BO performance and how to properly address them for best results.

In this sense, one of the first choices that were made referred to the method selected for the parameter optimisation of the GP kernel fitted on the datasets for surrogate modelling of the objective functions. As described by Roman et al. in their 2019 paper focused on kernel selection for Bayesian Optimisation [1], the two most common ways to optimise these GP kernel parameters are by optimising the likelihood function (abbrev. in the paper as ‘OPT’), or _via_ the Markov Chain Monte Carlo approach (abbrev. in the paper as ‘MCMC’) [1]. After they performed a comparison study between the two methods, they concluded that OPT yielded better results than MCMC when applied to real-world problems, and this is the method they used to perform the rest of the work in this paper [1]. For this reason, I also chose to use the optimisation of the likelihood function (OPT) [1] consistently as the GP kernel parameter optimisation method over the course of the BBO capstone project. More specifically, I made use of SciPy library’s native optimizer L-BFGS-B from scipy.optimize, as this is the most stable and efficient optimizer (which is also set as default) for internal GP parameter optimisation. As I used the scikit-learn library consistently as the core of my BBO capstone approach, due to its excellent performance on small-sized datasets, useful ML tools and its internal use of SciPy library’s optimizers (including the chosen L-BFGS-B), the GP used for fitting was always scikit-learn library’s GaussianProcessRegressor. Starting in Week 6 and for use in all future work, I improved a few key aspects related to the GP fitting process: (1) I ensured full reproducibility is always achieved by implementing random_state=42, and (2) I improved the parameter optimisation performance of the optimizer by implementing n_restarts_optimizer=10, rather than the default single optimizer run (n_restarts_optimizer=0), to mitigate the risk of the optimizer getting stuck into local optima. 

Starting with Week 6, another decision I made was to conduct a (A) standalone GP kernel ablation study, followed by a (B) standalone acqusition function ablation study, to automate the kernel and acqusition function selection process for each black-box function, make it more robust and efficient, and to avoid wasting expensive budget by manually testing potentially good choices one at a time, as it was performed during the initial very exploratory stages of the capstone project in Weeks 1-5. The choice to optimise the selection of surrogate model’s kernel and acqusition function for each function was made because these two are the most important components of a Bayesian Optimisation pipeline and main drivers of either BO success or failure per function. 

For the function-specific GP kernel ablation study (A), Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) R² was used as the chosen metric to assess and rank the kernels based on their predictive out-of-sample surrogate modelling performance. Using LOOCV R² is, to the best of my knowledge, a novel method for function-specific GP kernel selection for Bayesian Optimisation and this idea was inspired by the work of R.A. Patil and E.R. Deore from 2026 [4]. This chosen method (LOOCV) and its corresponding metric (R²) leverages the small-sized datasets of black-box functions to identify, for each function, the GP kernel which is able to predict the location of a held-out-point (of known true output), based on training data, as it is the case (ideally) for the surrogate modelling of objective unknown functions, when the GP is only trained on the small-sized true datasets that describe them and would ideally be able to extrapolate (within reason and with some uncertainty) to new areas as accurately as possible. It was thought that a GP with a kernel that is able to model the objective unknown function well will consequently predict the position of a new, unseen datapoint in the output space with good accuracy after being trained on real but sparse black-box function data (i.e. yielding a high LOOCV R²) and will be considered the highest-quality GP kernel for that specific function, selected for use in next query prediction generation via Bayesian Optimisation.

For the function-specific acqusition ablation study (B) I performed in Week 6 (following the kernel ablation study I described above), the mean leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV) rank‑percentile was implemented as the performance metric used for acquisition function ranking, which was noted in the following week (Week 7) that this is actually not the ideal acquisition performance metric to choose when the goal of the acquisition ablation study is to identify the best acquisition function which (ideally) leads to global function maximisation (by identifying the highest-quality next query point _via_ Bayesian Optimisation). While this metric was indeed not the most inspired one to use, the overall BBO Week 6 pipeline still performed well and managed to further maximise 4 out of the 8 black-box functions, specifically Functions 2, 4, 5, 6. To improve the acquisition function ablation study in Week 7, a robust multi-step sequential offline Bayesian Optimization rollout was performed, where the performance metric used for acquisition function ranking was long-term cumulative regret, as mentioned in some of the existing literature [5] studying acquisition function optimisation for BO.

In Week 6 of the capstone project, to build the design and development towards a more advanced and effective future BO pipeline, I made use of some ideas from the paper titled “A Simple Test for Heteroscedasticity and Random Coefficient Variation”, published in 1979 by T. S. Breusch and A. R. Pagan [2], as well as from the paper titled “HEBO: Pushing the Limits of Sample‑Efficient Hyperparameter Optimisation” paper published in 2022 by A. I. Cowen‑Rivers et al. [3]. The former [2] describes a fast and simple statistical test used to identify whether a function is homo- or heteroscedastic (i.e. if it is characterised by either constant or non-constant variance). This is important, because the surrogate model that was selected for use in the capstone project to model the objective black-box functions (GP) assumes homoscedastic function behaviour, and any non-constant variance in the unknown functions’ behaviour would directly compromise the objective function modelling performance and therefore next query predictions. The heteroscedasticity test described in the paper is now called a Breusch-Pagan test (following the authors’ names). In the context of this capstone project, it was implemented in a modified way, by plotting the residuals calculated as the difference between the actual observed datapoints and the output values predicted by the function-specific GPs (instead of computing the standard OLS residuals used in the Breusch-Pagan test). The shape of the scatter plot and the computed p-value acted as a diagnostic on whether heteroscedastic behaviour may be present within the unknown objective functions. If heteroscedasticity is noted as a result of this Breusch-Pagan-style analysis, heteroscedasticity will have to be addressed as part of the BBO capstone project and BO pipeline. A way to deal with this (which was employed starting with the following week, i.e. Week 7) is by employing a method proposed in the second paper mentioned, by A. I. Cowen‑Rivers et al. from Noah’s Ark Lab at Huawei [3], where they dealt with possible heteroscedasticies within the data by using non-linear parametric output warping. This encompasses two complementary methods, specifically the Box-Cox (which only works when output values > 0) and Yeo Johnson (which also works for output values that are <= 0) transformations, that apply parametric non-linear transformations to the function’s output, to reduce heteroscedasticity and stabilise variance before surrogate fitting [3], enabling the GP to more accurately model the objective functions. As part of Week 7 of the capstone project, where I implemented this HEBO-style non-linear parametric output warping [3], the functions’ outputs were then also unwarped at the end, after next query prediction generation, so that the predicted output values of the generated next query points (1 per black-box function) are printed at the original scales and can be directly compared with the output values within the existing true function datasets.


_Continuous Strategic BO ML Pipeline Refinement:_

Starting with Week 9, I went on to implement the recent research by F. Zhang and Y. Chen, titled “Direct Regret Optimization in Bayesian Optimization” published in 2025 [6]. This paper focuses its basis on the idea that usually, in Bayesian Optimisation, the selection of surrogate models and acquisition functions is conducted separately, and this disconnect between two essential parts of BO that then work together to achieve the main goal of minimising simple regret, may lead to suboptimal results. This idea is also mentioned and supported, as well as investigated further by other recent papers [7,8] by J.-H. Park et al. The first paper mentioned in this section by F. Zhang and Y. Chen [6] proposes, describes and demonstrates that the simultaneous selection of surrogate model and acquisition function as a joint pair for each expensive objective function is a more robust strategy than previous BO baselines that separately choose the two components by effectively leading to highly superior outcomes and lower simple regret values when chosen together. This study shows that by taking into account the intrinsic interaction between the surrogate model and the acquisition function during component selection, _via_ benchmarking of their performance as a whole working pair (with the main performance metric being simple regret) rather than separately, the BO settings for each objective function achieve improved optimisation, which translates to better performance in the real world – i.e. the winning surrogate model-acquisition function pair generating next query predictions of improved quality _via_ BO. 

In Week 6, the surrogate model kernel ablation and the acquisition function ablation took place separately, as two unrelated and disconnected optimisation tasks, each of them outputting an optimal setting (kernel or acquisition function) for each black-box function, as considered and selected by chosen performance metrics. This strategy was later (in Week 9) modified and improved as per the papers mentioned above [6,7,8], so that only a single full joint kernel x acqusition function ablation study is performed, to fully inspect the kernel-acquisition function combinations as whole systems (that consider their interaction) outputting a single performance metric used for ranking, i.e. mean simple final regret, across multiple seeds (AURC was used as a secondary ranking metric used as a tiebreaker). This strategic pipeline improvement (i.e. the single joint kernel x acqusition ablation study) was not implemented immediately (i.e. in Week 7), as, at that stage, the BO pipeline still required some more tuning and improvement until this more high-level systematic amendment made sense to be employed. For example, in Week 6, within the newly implemented independent acqusition function ablation study, the acquisition function performance metric used for ranking was the mean leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV) rank‑percentile, which (as I also mentioned in above) is not actually the best performance metric to use for the ranking and selection of acqusition functions for BO when the main goal of the overall process is to achieve global function maximisation. Therefore, as mentioned before, in Week 7, this issue had to be dealt with immediately first, by implementing a proper multi-step sequential offline Bayesian Optimization rollout study, using long-term cumulative regret as the performance metric this time, which much better and accurately reflects the goal that the project is aiming for, and was implemented in other studies [5] related to acqusition function selection for BO.

Once the joint kernel x acquisition function ablation study was implemented in Week 9, the pipeline became more robust and statistically reliable in Week 10, through the addition of Standard Errors (SEM) and 95% Confidence Intervals (t-distribution, computed across seeds) uncertainty estimates to the Mean Simple Final Regret primary performance metric (Selected to align with the central optimisation goal of this project - global function maximisation) and utilised in recent advancements in the literature [6]. 

The pipeline was then further developed in Week 11, by implmenting a K-Means Clustering-Informed Local Box Definition as a new BBO pipeline step between the joint ablation study and next-query prediction, for the identification of the most promising region to sample from for the overarching goal of global function maximisation, _via_ unsupervised learning. For each function, K-means was swept across a range of k values, with the best number of clusters (k) selected _via_ a distance-to-chord elbow heuristic.

An advanced Density-Enhanced K-Means Clustering-Informed Local-Box Sobol sampling strategy was added at the same time, to enrich the existing full-space Sobol sampling strategy utilised so far, by leveraging the additional knowledge regarding the best K-Means cluster region to sample from for each function for the goal of global function maximisation. Two separate Sobol candidate sampling pools were generated for acquisition scoring and next-query prediction: the already used full-domain Sobol candidate pool, and a density-enhanced K-Means clustering-informed local-box Sobol candidate pool. The local-box Sobol candidate density enhancement was performed in a controlled manner to avoid introducing bias, by initially density-matching the local-box pool with the full-space pool for each function (accounting for the box's actual volume, computed in log-space for numerical stability at higher dimensions), then applying a preset resolution boost on top of that density-matched baseline. A floor and a cap were also used to keep small/large boxes within good performance limits. The GP was still trained on the full dataset for each function, but the final candidate was chosen by argmax over the combined Sobol pools, so that the most promising region (defined _via_ the computed best K-Means cluster) gets denser candidate resolution without removing the rest of the domain from consideration. In this way, the BO pipeline remains fully flexible and non-myopic to the full space, while leveraging the most promising regions that seem to be surrounding a maximum. This should maximise the chances of predicting a very high-quality next query point per function and achieving the goal of efficient global function maximisation.

Finally, in the last week (13) of the BBO capstone project, a highly-exploitative approach was taken to leverage all collected function information so far, in the past 12 weeks, as well as the most robust, statistically reliable, transparent and interpretable BO ML pipeline so far, the one used in Weeks 11 and 12. The only modification for Week 13 targeted the selected acquisition functions to be included in the kernel x acquisition ablation sweep, for which only the 3 most exploitative acquisition functions settings were chosen.
